# Literature Review — Track 1: Philosophy of Mind / AI Welfare

Status: working document, last updated Week 6 (2026-07-31). Consolidated from the research log; will keep growing as the review continues.

## Purpose in this project

Track 1 supplies the grounding for why the existential-gap question (is there a continuous someone bearing the outcome of an AI system's actions) is a poor foundation for policy — and why a weaker claim, causal/psychological continuity of a mind-stream rather than metaphysical selfhood, is defensible and sufficient for the accountability-continuity criterion this project actually uses.

## Foundational sources (Weeks 1-2)

- **Nagel (1974), "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"** — grounds the existential-gap claim: consciousness involves a subjective, first-person "what it is like," not reducible to third-person functional description.
- **Chalmers, the hard problem of consciousness / philosophical zombies** — the thought experiment that a system could be functionally identical to a conscious being while lacking subjective experience; used to keep the behavioral gap and existential gap conceptually separate.
- **Buddhist philosophy — 无我 (anatta), 心相续 / 阿赖耶識 (mind-stream continuity)** and **Parfit (1984), *Reasons and Persons*** — both deny a persistent metaphysical self, even in humans, which initially threatened this project's premise. Resolved by weakening the claim from metaphysical selfhood to **causal continuity of a mind-stream**, a claim both traditions accept. AI's retrieval-based memory provides *informational* continuity (a record of experience) but not this *causal* continuity (the experience itself, carried forward) — illustrated with an amnesia-patient/case-file analogy (see `/essays/the-gap-that-cant-close.md`).

## The markers-based AI welfare/consciousness literature (Week 4-6)

- **Long, Sebo, Butlin, Plunkett et al. (2026), "Studying AI Welfare Empirically"** (Eleos AI / NYU CMEP) — the field's current best-practice methodology paper. Its "entities" section (model vs. persona vs. instance vs. instance-persona vs. forward pass) independently arrives at a Parfit-style move to handle AI entity individuation. Its own catalog of challenges (mismatch problem, specificity problem, solution space problem, **anchor problem**) is the direct source of this project's live open concern.
- **Butlin, Long, Bayne, Bengio, Birch, Chalmers, Constant, Deane, Elmoznino, Fleming, Ji, Kanai, Klein, Lindsay, Michel, Mudrik, Peters, Schwitzgebel, Simon & VanRullen, "Identifying indicators of consciousness in AI systems"** (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2025/2026) — the direct methodological ancestor of the Long/Sebo/Butlin/Plunkett paper above. Derives indicator properties from multiple competing theories of consciousness (global workspace, IIT, recurrent processing, higher-order, predictive processing, attention schema) rather than committing to one, producing a probabilistic rather than binary assessment tool.

## The open disagreement: the anchor problem

This project's standing, unresolved concern: whether markers-based AI consciousness research can ever be evidentially decisive, given AI systems lack the evolutionary/anatomical anchor that licenses behavior-to-inner-state inference in animal welfare science. Recorded as an open disagreement with the marker-based research program, not a settled objection.

Two independent sources found in Week 6 converge on this same worry:

- **Koch (2026), "From indicators to biology: the calibration problem in artificial consciousness"** (arXiv:2603.27597) — argues the indicator-based program is "epistemically under-calibrated": indicators lack independent validation and no ground truth of artificial phenomenality exists, so probabilistic consciousness attribution to current AI systems is premature. Koch's own proposed fix (redirect toward biologically-grounded engineering — biohybrid, neuromorphic, connectome-scale systems) doesn't solve this project's problem, since this project isn't trying to build biologically-anchored AI — but the critique itself is independent confirmation that the anchor problem is a live, named concern within the indicators literature itself, not just this project's own skepticism.
- **Schwitzgebel (2026), "AI and Consciousness"** (arXiv:2510.09858, skeptical overview; Cambridge Elements monograph forthcoming August 2026) — central verdict: "none of the standard arguments either for or against AI consciousness takes us far." Chapter Seven, "The Mimicry Argument Against AI Consciousness," directly supports the worry that behavioral markers may be systematically confoundable by mimicry rather than genuine indicators.

## Working conclusion

These three sources (Long/Sebo/Butlin/Plunkett, Koch, Schwitzgebel) converge on a reading that *strengthens* rather than undermines this project's institutional-design move (see `/framework-drafts/two-axis-framework.md`): treating accountability continuity AC(O,t) as a hard constraint decided on grounds other than resolving the underlying metaphysics is the right response to a calibration problem that may be durable, not a workaround pending better science.
