# Easier to check, or easier to believe?

*Research and product note · November 2026*

In earlier research, I checked AI-generated classification codes against official sources. A code could look plausible until you read the rule it was supposed to follow. That experience gave me a question I pitched at Foresight Vision Weekend: **If we put the evidence beside an AI recommendation, does it become easier to check or just easier to believe?**

It’s one part of a larger question I care about: what makes an AI-supported workplace a place where people can still exercise judgment, learn from their decisions, and take responsibility for them?

## The first test: where the evidence appears

In the [proposed A/B test](../studies/organizational-coordination/evidence-access-pilot/README.md), one group would see the relevant passage beside the recommendation. The other could reveal the same passage with one click. Both would have access to the full source. Showing the passage also draws attention to it, so the test can’t tell us whether any difference comes from saving a click or from making the evidence more noticeable.

I’d count how often people catch an error and how often they send a correct recommendation back. If both go up, reviewers may just be rejecting more recommendations. I’d also ask which fact drove their decision. That gives me a better clue about what they noticed.

## When is an extra step worth it?

A separate idea is to require reviewers to open the source before they can approve a recommendation. I think of this as *positive friction*, if it earns the name. A pause might help someone catch an error. It could also become one more box to tick.

To test that idea, I’d keep the evidence and layout the same and compare an optional source check with a required one. I’d want to know whether the extra step improves decisions and how much time it adds. A click records an action; it doesn’t show that anyone read or understood the source.

## What could be useful in practice?

One possible starting point is a review panel for research or policy teams that already check AI-drafted analyses. It could put the source next to the draft and let a reviewer flag an uncertain claim, explain what needs checking, and pass that note to someone who can act on it. The handoff matters: spotting a problem is of little use if it’s unclear who can fix it.

Before building a product, I’d ask reviewers to walk me through the last recommendation they sent back. Where did they lose time? What happened after they flagged it? If the same problem keeps coming up, I’d want to see what their current tools already do well, what a prototype could improve, and who would pay for that improvement.

**Where this stands:** The repository has fictional cases, assignment code, and analysis checks using synthetic data for the first comparison. No participants have taken part. The required review step and the review panel are ideas to explore; demand hasn’t been tested.
