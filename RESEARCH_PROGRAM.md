# Human Judgment Feedback and Learning with AI Assistance

**Wenwen (Celine) Zhang · Working research program · Updated October 1, 2026**

I want to understand whether judgment, feedback, and responsibility remain
connected as more work is delegated to AI. Do people encounter the consequences
of decisions, have the ability to respond, and use what they learn in later work?
This includes both individual development and the conditions for organizations
to retain experience. The branches below examine different parts of that concern.

## Purpose and evaluation

This concern also asks who defines a good outcome and whether experience can
change the aim itself. Organizations may pursue different purposes, and people
within one organization may disagree. A single metric cannot settle these
choices. The [October 1 reflection](docs/purpose-judgment-and-accountability.md)
develops this philosophical motivation. It adds questions for future study, not
new findings or a new score imposed on the existing cases.

## Two related questions

The AC simulation asks a measurement question: can an organization distinguish
better AI from reduced human review using the rate at which people override
AI? Under a stated model, the same rate can arise from different processes.
Additional information helps only with explicit assumptions.

The proposed experiment asks an intervention question: does recording an
initial judgment before AI advice affect later unaided performance? This
cannot be answered by the simulation. A randomly assigned workflow, a latent
review process, and an observed disagreement are different variables.

## Proposed first study

Compare three workflows on repeated cases with verified feedback:

| Workflow | Before final submission |
|---|---|
| Initial Judgment First | Examine evidence, record an answer and confidence, see prepared AI advice, then retain or revise the answer |
| AI Advice First | Examine the same evidence with the same prepared advice available, then answer |
| No AI | Examine the same evidence and answer without advice, with an opportunity to review |

All three groups receive the same correct-answer feedback under the same rules.
The primary comparison is later unaided accuracy in Initial Judgment First
versus AI Advice First. The No AI group provides an additional benchmark.
Immediate performance, confidence, and responses to correct and wrong advice
are separate outcomes.

The [operational draft](studies/ai-advice-timing/DRAFT_PROTOCOL.md) specifies a
proposed sequence, measures, analysis, and decisions still to be reviewed. The
[example cases](studies/ai-advice-timing/TASK_EXAMPLES.md) use fictional evidence
review to make the task concrete. Neither document is a validated instrument
or a completed pilot.

The initial feasibility option uses adults. The [youth proposal](preserving-independent-judgment-in-youth-ai-use.md)
addresses a setting of particular interest, but requires separate adaptation,
partners, and research review. It is not assumed that adult results would
generalize to young people.

## Why the answer is open

A recorded initial answer could give feedback a distinct judgment to correct.
It could also make a person reluctant to abandon an error. Early AI advice
could direct attention to useful evidence or discourage independent checking.
Changes in effort, timing, and commitment come together in these workflows.
The first comparison would estimate their combined effect, not isolate one
psychological mechanism.

Previous experiments motivate separating immediate decisions from learning,
and agreement from appropriate use of advice. They do not establish that
judgment first is always preferable. See the [focused review](ai-advice-timing-and-human-learning.md)
and the narrower source-to-design links in the [operational draft](studies/ai-advice-timing/DRAFT_PROTOCOL.md#evidence-informing-this-draft).

## An initial organizational evidence branch

Even if a person learns, an organization may not retain that knowledge. The
[October 1 public-document pilot](studies/organizational-feedback/README.md)
asks which steps from a reported problem to an organizational response can be
documented. It records local adjustments, implemented shared changes, plans,
requests, and unresolved evidence links separately.

This descriptive branch does not estimate effects on team capability,
institutional accountability, or professional performance. Studying whether
feedback reaches the original decision-maker, another reviewer, or a shared
resource and improves later work still requires linked process and outcome data.
Neither the advice-timing study nor this first coding exercise tests the entire
AC framework.

## Current status

The available work is an illustrative simulation, a set of study designs, and
an exploratory coding pilot using public institutional reports. There are no
participants or experimental results from this project. AI assistance contributed
substantially to the design and implementation; the [status and contribution
note](RESEARCH_STATUS.md) describes the division between completed work,
hypotheses, and future plans.
