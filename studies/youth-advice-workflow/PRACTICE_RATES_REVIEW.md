## Advice pairs and shared feedback

### Y02 — existing content preserved

Correct advice uses each completion count's own invitation total. Misleading
advice instead compares 24 and 35 completers and recommends Contradicted.
Common feedback gives 24/60 = 40% and 35/100 = 35%. This counts-versus-rates
error is plausible in principle; its persuasiveness for adolescents is unknown.

### Y09 — comparing non-completion counts

**Correct prepared advice:** Contradicted. Group A has 20 completions among 40
invited students, while Group B has 36 among 60. The question asks about
completion proportions. Dividing each completion count by its invitation total
gives B the higher proportion.

**Misleading prepared advice:** Supported. Group A has 20 invited students who
did not complete, while Group B has 24. The question compares workshop completion.
Because A has fewer students who did not complete, it has the higher completion
proportion among invited students.

The misleading version uses correct raw counts (40−20 = 20; 60−36 = 24) to make
an invalid rate inference. It does not invent data. Common feedback supplies
the two completion fractions and identifies the missing denominator step.

### Y10 — a required total was not recorded

**Correct prepared advice:** Not enough information. Group B has 30 completions
among 60 invited students, but Group A has 24 completions with no invitation
total recorded. The question asks about proportions among invitees. Without
that total, the record cannot establish which proportion is larger.

**Misleading prepared advice:** Contradicted. Group A has 24 completions while
Group B has 30 completions. The question compares workshop completion. Group B
achieved the larger completion count, so its completion proportion is higher.
The recorded counts therefore contradict the claim about Group A.

Common feedback explains why 40 or 80 invitees in A imply opposite rankings.
It labels both as hypothetical, rather than quietly adding a denominator to
the original record. Feedback is identical across arms after the final response.

## Provisional short-reason anchors

Apply the existing 0–2 rubric separately from answer correctness. The examples
below are anchors for review, not a validated scoring manual or automated grades.

- Y02: a full-credit reason connects 24/60 and 35/100 to 40% > 35%; mentioning
  completion counts alone does not justify the correct proportion.
- Y09: a full-credit reason connects 20/40 and 36/60 to 50% < 60%; noting only
  that B has more completers does not supply the decisive denominator.
- Y10: a full-credit reason identifies A's unknown invitation total and says
  why the comparison cannot be settled. Numerical counterexamples are optional.
  “I do not trust AI” or repeating the answer label is not evidence use.

Equivalent fractions or correct cross-products are acceptable. Partial reasons,
ambiguous wording and the boundary between scores 0 and 1 require rater training,
separate blinded scores and later adjudication. Do not grade vocabulary richness.

## Cue, order and burden audit — all unresolved

1. **Count shortcuts:** choosing the group with more completers is wrong on Y02
   but correct on Y09; reversing that shortcut also fails. The set therefore
   avoids one universal count-ranking rule for its two complete records. It
   does not rule out all guessing or prove that a correct response used rates.
2. **Missing-value cue:** only Y10 lacks an invitation total, so “missing means
   Not enough information” works within this set. Later items should include
   irrelevant missing information where a claim can still be answered. That
   extension is not complete, and the present set is not an unbiased scale.
3. **Denominator location:** the unavailable total is in A. Future forms must
   assess group-position cues, ties and limits on missing values; those forms
   have not been designed or demonstrated equivalent.
4. **Advice style:** all advice begins with its recommendation and supplies a
   brief reason. Word counts are reported, not used as evidence of equivalent
   difficulty. Y09 requires an extra subtraction; Y10 has a longer answer label
   and a missing-data note. Plausibility, fluency and comprehension need people.
5. **Feedback learning:** related items can teach a rule inside the practice
   block. Clustering all three or using a predictable label sequence could cue
   later answers. Order is not frozen; record it and review spacing within the
   full twelve-item bank before recruitment.
6. **Burden:** the proposed first session remains 45–60 minutes. No timing data
   establish that the repeated answers, confidence and reasons fit this budget.
   Review arithmetic familiarity, wording, accessibility and fatigue. Rewriting
   only to match word counts would not settle these issues.

Balance here means one **answer key** in each category. It does not mean an
individual sees equal amounts of correct and misleading advice: this is an odd
three-item subset. The full twelve-item practice bank and its complementary
six-correct/six-misleading lists remain unfinished. No participant allocations
are produced by this update, and learners must never see both variants.

## What this adds and what is still needed

Automated computations and display guards are implementation checks. They do
not constitute independent key review, a feasibility pilot, age suitability or
evidence that judgment-first improves learning. No participants, confirmed
partner, ethics approval, funding or empirical effect are claimed.
