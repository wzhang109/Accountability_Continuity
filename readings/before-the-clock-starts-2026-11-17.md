Can a public source show how a team made an earlier wait visible, and what remains unknown even with two timestamps? An emergency-department intake study offers a comparison from another setting. ## A handoff before formal triage

At one Boston hospital, an observer logged arrival time and the presenting complaint on a shared dashboard when triage stations were occupied. Formal triage retained its usual timestamp. The study describes two routes that bypassed this pre-triage step. Emergency medical service radio calls went directly to a bed with physician and nursing staff, and other arrivals generally went directly to triage when a station was available. The authors report 50,576 visits, of which 7,795 waited for triage. In that subgroup the median interval was 11 minutes, with an interquartile range of 5–19 minutes. The two endpoints expose time before routine triage. The paper does not report when a specific receiving clinician saw the dashboard entry, how much time staff spent preparing or acting on it, or an effect on patient outcomes. [Houston et al., 2015 issue, Methods, Results and Limitations, pp. 40–41](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4gj1g690)

## A longer wait at one stage can coexist with a shorter journey

In a separate observational study across four UK acute-care sites, researchers followed 108 patients for whom the admission decision was initially unclear. They coded patient-visible activity and waiting, with a distinct category for waits whose reason was unknown. The site with the longest initial wait had comparatively short assessment and treatment times. The authors report a shorter average overall journey at that site. This contrast is descriptive. Patient paths and site arrangements differed, and a pause experienced by a patient could coincide with work elsewhere. The source does not measure staff labor during those pauses. [Swancutt et al., 2017, Methods, Results and Discussion](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12913-017-2349-2)

## What to record in a future review

For a review sent back for correction, record when the issue was first noticed if independently observable, who put it in the shared queue, who received or accepted it, and when the decision was made. The gap between posting and acceptance measures a queue interval only if acceptance is visible and actually means a role took responsibility. A gap between two record entries can also contain unobserved work. Measure active work separately, by role and method, allowing effort by multiple people to overlap. Preserve a route with no queue, distinguish observed zero from unknown, and allow an issue to end without a formal report.
