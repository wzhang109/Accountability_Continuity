# Preparation, assignment and reported completion

November 3, 2026 · Bounded source reading; AI assisted

The [October 29 note](requests-and-authority-2026-10-29.md) separated an
evidence request from authorization and execution. This reading asks whether
the same stages always occur in that order. It does not settle that question.

## Source and evidence boundary

Kotlarsky, van den Hooff and Geerts, *Under pressure: Understanding the dynamics
of coordination in IT functions under business-as-usual and emergency
conditions*, DOI [10.1177/0268396219881461](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0268396219881461).
The publisher confirms first online publication on October 29, 2019 and volume
35(2). The accessible text is an [author-uploaded online-first copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/336894483_Under_pressure_Understanding_the_dynamics_of_coordination_in_IT_functions_under_business-as-usual_and_emergency_conditions),
with internal pages 1–29, not a verified match to final journal pagination.

The methods report 33 interviews in two units of one IT service organization,
alongside meeting observations and documents (internal pp. 6–8). Those broader
methods do not make every quoted event a directly observed event. The records
below are interview accounts reproduced by the authors; raw transcripts, work
orders and delivery logs were not independently checked.

## Two bounded contrasts

| Record | What the source reports | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| C1, Table 3, internal p. 7 | Cooling-equipment replacement remained unresolved amid an expired contract, tendering and low priority; a later breakdown prompted emergency repair activity. | Whether replacement had formal approval, measured delay attributable to each constraint, and independently verified repair outcome. |
| M1, Episode 1, internal p. 13 | An informal alert preceded preparation; a formal assignment followed, then equipment dispatch was reported. | Standing permission for preparation, precise activity boundaries, resource costs, receipt and operational result. |

The M1 participant attributed timely dispatch to early preparation. That
counterfactual is a participant's assessment, not an estimated effect. The
military-operation context is not an ordinary office-work success case merely
because the authors place it in their normal-conditions section. C1 likewise
does not establish an approved-but-stalled request.

## What changes in the next observation

Record **what action began**: gathering information, making reversible
preparations, committing resources, dispatching, or accepting completed work.
For each action, separately record the relevant permission or assignment,
whether it is documented or unknown, and what evidence supports the outcome.
No formal assignment yet must not be coded automatically as no permission.

This distinction weakens a proposed single request → approval → action sequence.
It does not show that preparation should precede approval, informal networks
outperform formal ones, hierarchy causes delay, or AI changes these relations.
These are proposed observation fields, not a validated scale or adopted design.

The search still lacks a linked routine-work comparison with documented
authorization and completed outcome. A useful next case would trace a normal
service request through work orders and receipt, or show preparation that was
wasted, prohibited or unsuccessful. That could challenge a preparation-first
story rather than accumulate favorable anecdotes.
